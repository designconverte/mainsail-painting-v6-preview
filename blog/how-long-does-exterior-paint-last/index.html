How Long Does Exterior Paint Last on a Cape Cod Home?

Research from the USDA’s Forest Products Laboratory puts paint on wood siding at about 7 to 10 years. Sun, water and preparation decide where your house lands in that range.

How long does exterior paint last? On wood siding, a well-applied paint job typically lasts 7 to 10 years, according to the USDA Forest Products Laboratory.[1] Where a house lands in that range depends on how many coats went on, how much sun and water each wall takes, and how well the surface was prepared before the first brush stroke.

How long does exterior paint last on wood siding?

Paint on wood siding typically lasts 7 to 10 years, longer than any other common exterior wood finish, according to the Forest Products Laboratory, the U.S. Forest Service’s wood research center. Its finishing factsheet gives these typical life spans:[1]

Life span of exterior wood finishes, from the USDA Forest Products Laboratory’s finishing factsheet. Ranges, not guarantees.
FinishTypical life span
Paint7–10 years
Solid-color stain3–7 years
Semitransparent stain3–8 years
Varnish2–3 years
Pigmented water-repellent preservative2–3 years
Clear water-repellent preservative1–2 years

The number of coats moves a house within that range. Purdue University’s extension guide to paint failure puts it plainly: “One top coat of paint should last four to five years, but two top coats can last up to 10 years.”[4] The laboratory describes the standard system as a primer and two top coats, 4 to 5 mils thick in all, “about the thickness of a sheet of newspaper.”[1]

The paint and the wood matter too. The laboratory notes that “a good acrylic latex outdoor house paint will generally outlast a good oil-base house paint.” Vertical-grain (edge-grain) boards shrink and swell about half as much as flat-grain boards, so paint has less reason to crack, and low-density woods such as western redcedar have “excellent properties for finishing.”[1]

Both sources are older research publications, so read their numbers as a baseline. For the paint on your house today, its manufacturer’s data sheet is the current word.

How often should you repaint a house exterior?

Repaint when the paint has weathered thin, not on a fixed calendar. The Forest Products Laboratory’s guidance is specific: “Ideally, wood should be repainted when the topcoats have weathered to the point that the primer is just starting to show. Painting too often can build up too many layers of paint.” It adds that “wood is easier to repaint before the first coat fails.”[1]

Not every wall wears at the same pace:

  • The north side “does not need to be repainted as often as other sides because it is exposed to less direct sunlight.” The laboratory suggests one top coat there instead of two, or painting it less often.[1]
  • Protected areas such as porch ceilings and roof overhangs should not be repainted as often as the rest of the house.[3]
  • South and west walls usually wear first, because they take the most sun and rain.[3]

Why not simply repaint everything every few years? Because paint that gets too thick stops moving with the wood. Laboratory researchers explain that cross-grain cracking “occurs when layers of paint become too thick” and lose their ability to shrink and expand. Once it happens, “the only solution is to remove the old paint completely and apply a new finish over the bare wood.”[3]

What shortens the life of exterior paint?

Three things, in roughly this order: water, sun and preparation.

Water

“Peeling paint is usually caused by water that gets between paint and the wood’s surface,” write the laboratory’s researchers. Leaky roofs, ice dams and short roof overhangs are common sources.[3] Purdue’s guide adds that “properly maintained gutters prevent rain water splash.”[4]

Some of the water comes from inside. When paint fails on the outside walls of a bathroom or kitchen, often worse on an upper floor, high indoor humidity is the likely cause; condensation on the windows is another sign. Turning down a humidifier or running the bathroom exhaust fan helps.[2]

Sun

Sunlight wears paint away over time, which is why the sunniest walls reach bare primer first. The areas that degrade fastest are “those exposed to the greatest amount of sun and rain, usually on the south and west sides of buildings.”[3]

Preparation

Paint can only hold on as well as the surface under it. Bare wood left out in the weather for two or three weeks already holds paint less well,[3] and new paint over a chalky, mildewed or dirty surface can separate from the old coat, a failure called intercoat peeling.[3] Purdue notes that intercoat peeling “usually results from inadequate cleaning of the weathered paint and usually occurs within one year of repainting.”[4]

How does Cape Cod’s weather affect exterior paint?

It adds water, and water is the main reason paint peels. Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution describes a climate moderated by the ocean, with “three to five days of fog each summer month along the shore” and a Northeaster season that “brings heavy rain and high winds and tides in any season.”[5] Wind-driven rain matters for paint: laboratory researchers found that paint failure “is most severe on the side of a building that faces the prevailing wind,” which pushes moisture into gaps and cracks.[3]

In practice, the walls that face the weather and the afternoon sun are the ones to inspect first each spring. For the weather on the painting day itself, see our guide to the best time to paint a house exterior on Cape Cod.

What are the signs it’s time to repaint?

The clearest sign is primer showing through on the sunniest walls; chalking, cracks across the grain, blisters, stains and soft wood follow. Walk the house on a clear day and look at each wall, then at the trim:

  1. Primer showing through on the sunniest walls. This is the point the laboratory calls ideal for repainting.[1]
  2. A chalky powder that comes off on your hand. Weathered paint turns powdery, and it has to be scrubbed off before a new coat will bond.[3]
  3. Cracks running across the boards instead of along the grain: a sign of too many layers.[3]
  4. Blisters or peeling down to bare wood. “Moisture blisters usually include all paint coats down to the wood surface.”[4] Find the water before repainting.
  5. Peeling only outside a bathroom, kitchen or upstairs room: usually indoor humidity.[2]
  6. Brown stains on cedar or redwood: moisture moving through the wood carries its natural extractives out through the paint.[2]
  7. Soft, spongy wood. Decayed wood “will never hold paint and should be replaced.”[2]
  8. Gray or dark discoloration. “Most gray discoloration is caused by mildew,” and it has to be cleaned off before painting.[1]

One or two of these on a single wall can mean a partial repaint. Several of them around the house usually mean it is time for the whole exterior.

Older homes: the lead-safe rule comes before the scraper

Built before 1978? Check the lead rules first

The EPA’s Renovation, Repair and Painting Rule covers houses built before 1978, and anyone paid to disturb paint in them must be certified.[6] In Massachusetts, paid work that disturbs more than 20 square feet of painted exterior surface “must be carried out by lead-safe renovation (LSR) contractor,” with an LSR supervisor on site at all times while the work is in progress.[7]

This matters most on exactly the houses that need a repaint. Thick, cracked paint on an older home has had decades to build up, and laboratory researchers caution that some paint showing cross-grain cracking is likely to be lead-based.[3] Before anyone scrapes or sands, ask for the contractor’s Massachusetts LSR license.

Frequently asked questions

01Can you paint over peeling paint?

Not directly. Loose paint has to come off first, and the laboratory’s advice is that any surface “should be sanded before it’s repainted.”[3] On a house built before 1978, the lead-safe rules apply before any scraping or sanding.[7]

02Why is my new exterior paint peeling after a year?

Usually because the old surface was not cleaned well enough. Intercoat peeling “usually results from inadequate cleaning of the weathered paint and usually occurs within one year of repainting.”[4]

03Is power washing enough to prepare a house for paint?

It is often the first step, not the whole job. The laboratory recommends power washing dirty siding or cleaning it with detergent and a stiff bristle brush, rinsing well, and never using steel or iron brushes, which can stain the wood.[2] Peeled areas still need scraping and sanding.[3]

04How can the next paint job last longer?

The laboratory’s summary: install siding properly, sand or power wash smooth wood, paint in the recommended weather, treat the wood with a paintable water-repellent preservative, prime with a stain-blocking primer, caulk properly and apply two latex top coats over the primer.[2]
